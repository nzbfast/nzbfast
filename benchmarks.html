Methodology first

The setup

  • Clients: nzbfast · SABnzbd 5.0.4 · NZBGet 26.2 · rustnzb 1.3.4 · Weaver v0.7.5, all on the same machine per scenario (Apple M-series boxes: "Europe" M1 Ultra on 10 GbE, "East Coast" M3 Ultra ~2.4-3.0 Gbps line). The headline provider legs (35, 77 and 190 GB) were re-measured 31 July 2026 on the shipping nzbfast build with all five clients, every leg over TLS. A few older legs remain and say so; they understate the shipping build rather than flatter it.
  • Fairness: same 6 providers × 8 connections, TLS everywhere, legs run back-to-back interleaved. Every competitor tuned to its documented best: SABnzbd got pipelining_requests=8 (it ships 1, i.e. unpipelined; that setting alone took its 190 GB time from 24m24s to 19m02s), NZBGet got ArticleCache/DirectWrite/DirectUnpack/ParQuick, rustnzb its documented config.
  • End state: a verified, extracted, usable file, not "download finished". That's what you actually wait for.
  • Reproducibility: every claim is dated and measured the same way: elapsed time + interface byte counters + process-tree peak RSS, 1-2 s resolution. Where a leg runs on a publicly posted NZB, we name it so you can run it yourself.

The census

Two shapes are 98.6% of the data

We looked at 890,852 releases, 1.6 million files and 79.6 TB across the two busiest movie and TV groups, then fetched and read the archive headers of a thousand real posts to confirm what the filenames only implied. Counted by bytes rather than by post, because a million tiny files matter less than one large one.

  • 2.5% of releases are multi-volume RAR archives, and they are 84% of all the data. Everything else is a long tail of small files.
  • Inside that: 84% stored (packed with no compression) and 14% encrypted. Together, 98.6%.
  • Compressed archives are 1.4%. Solid archives, the slowest kind to unpack, turned out to be extinct: not one in roughly 700 archives we read.

That reshaped what we work on. There is little point tuning a compression path that carries 1.4% of the data, so we tuned the two that carry the rest.

Encryption is not spread evenly either. It scales with size:

Release sizeShare of all dataStoredEncrypted
1-5 GB29%94%2%
5-20 GB39%97%2%
20-60 GB20%67%33%
over 60 GB12%51%49%

Ordinary downloads are almost always plain stored archives. The big ones are a coin flip between stored and encrypted. Below is how nzbfast handles each.

The honest column

The legs we don't win

Every leg that has sat in this section has eventually been re-measured on a newer shipping build, and this round emptied it, which is worth being careful rather than pleased about. What left, each explained beside its own table rather than quietly edited: the heaviest PAR2 repair, which spent two cuts of this page as a loss on two machines; rebuilding missing .rev recovery volumes, the largest loss this page has carried at 6.6× slower than RARLab's own tool and now a narrow win; and three extraction shapes that were a few percent to a third behind unrar, where the cause turned out to be narrower than the explanation we published for it.

What has not gone away is the trade behind those numbers, so that is what this section says now: we spend more memory than the standalone tools do, and the heaviest repair now spends more than it used to. Our extractor and repairer are built to ride a live download rather than to run once from a command line, and that costs resident memory; the detail is beside the component tables. If your constraint is the smallest possible footprint for a one-shot job, the dedicated tools win that column and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

Why show a loss at all? Because the wins are only believable next to it. Every number on this page comes from the same interleaved runs, and a leg we lose stays published until a re-run replaces it.

Two are easy to get wrong and worth knowing if you run your own: SABnzbd rewrites its own ini at startup and reverts pipelining_requests to the shipped 1, so pipelining has to be set over the API afterwards and read back to confirm; and a standalone NZBGet -c config inherits NZBGet's built-in defaults rather than the values in its shipped conf, which silently leaves the article cache off. Shape one · 84% of the data

Plain stored archives: a queue of jobs

Three real releases, queued one after another so each waits for the previous to finish. That is how a queue actually drains, and it is where the cost of starting each job shows up. Six providers, the same connection count for every client, same machine and network.

nzbfast is 1.51x faster than NZBGet and 2.55x faster than SABnzbd on the single most common shape on usenet.

Every client was checked at 8, 16 and 32 connections per server and is shown at its own best, because a competitor should never lose for being badly configured. Worth knowing if you tune your own setup: NZBGet got about twice as slow at 32 connections, and SABnzbd behaved the same way in our earlier testing. We also check that each client actually produced the files, rather than trusting it when it reports success. One client in our testing reported three completed downloads in about a second each and had written nothing at all.

Shape two · 14% of the data, half of everything over 60 GB

Encrypted archives, and half the writing

On a real 94 GB encrypted release, nzbfast now writes 90 GB to your disk where it used to write 180 GB, and finishes the moment the download does instead of pausing for a separate unlock pass.

Encrypted archives were the last shape we could not handle in a single pass. nzbfast had to write the locked data to disk, read it all back, unlock it, and write it out again. Twice the writing, twice the disk, and a wait at the end that grew with the release. It now unlocks each piece as it arrives, so the locked data never reaches the disk at all.

The shape of it

Disk in use during one download, sampled every five seconds. The two lines are identical right up to the moment the download finishes. Then the old one climbs to 166 GB: that is the unlock pass, writing the finished file while the locked copy is still on disk. The new line simply stops, because the file it was writing was already unlocked.

Why it matters

Your drive does half the work

Flash storage wears out by being written to. Downloading a 94 GB release used to cost your drive 180 GB of writing; now it costs 90 GB. On a NAS with hard disks the same change removes a long single-threaded pass at the end of every encrypted download, which on a slower machine takes minutes rather than the 20 seconds we measured here.

What we are not claiming

  • The 20 second pause we measured is the best case for the old behaviour. It was measured on a fast 32-core workstation with hardware acceleration for the unlocking. On a low-power NAS, which is where most people run this, the old pass is far slower and the improvement correspondingly larger. We quote the smaller number because it is the one we measured.
  • The 5.6% faster finish is real but modest, and it is entirely the removed pause at the end. The download itself is not faster. In our runs the new behaviour's download leg was in fact marginally slower than the old one, which is why we are confident the 5.6% is not measurement drift flattering us.

Component shootouts

The more technical benchmarks, for people who like more data

Repair (PAR2) and unpack (RAR) are our own native code rather than bundled third-party binaries, so we also race them standalone against the dedicated tools on identical corpora, on four machines spanning what a reader might actually own. A time only counts when the output is byte-identical to the source payload: every RAR figure below was sha256-checked against the source, and every repaired file against the pristine set.

4 machines, laptop to 32-core 7 RAR archive shapes 6 extractors raced 1 GB of payload per shape best of 3 interleaved, warm cache

RAR extraction: 7 archive shapes, 6 tools

The previous round of this table used 100 MB to 200 MB per shape, which was a mistake: about 28 ms of process launch was 40% of the store leg, and the ordering it produced does not survive at a realistic size. This round is 1 GB of payload per shape, and it changes several answers, including some in the other direction. Archives are created by official rar 7.23, so no tool is judged on input from its own encoder, and the same bytes are raced on every machine.

What is in the payload matters more than it looks. A payload built out of block copies makes every compressed shape a memory-copy benchmark; a payload of pure text makes it a literal-and-Huffman benchmark; we measured both and they do not agree on who wins. So the four compressed shapes use equal thirds of text, structured records and incompressible bytes, and the two shapes at the ends of that range are separate legs on purpose: store is incompressible and repetitive is almost all matches. The builder and the harness are in the repository, so the corpus can be rebuilt byte for byte.

Every shape on every machine except one, and that one is a tie. The previous cut of this page lost five of these cells and the reason they moved is worth stating, because it is not the reason we first gave. We blamed the platform's memory-copy routine, and the profile said something narrower: a match of two to thirty-two bytes was paying for a full call into that routine, and the call cost more than the copy. Copying a fixed thirty-two bytes through a register instead removed it, which is why repetitive, solid and the 128 MiB dictionary all moved at once - they are the three shapes built out of short matches. Separately the checksum was running on the writer's thread and holding it up; moving it downstream took the rest. The one cell that is not ours is the stored shape on the 32-core desktop, where unrar and we are three milliseconds apart on a leg that is purely moving bytes.

The shape we win by the most is the one usenet actually posts hundreds of at a time: 400 small files, 4.3× and 4.4× against unrar and 5.4× to 5.5× against rarpar. That is per-member parallelism, and it is the difference between an extractor written for a download queue and one written for a command line. The stored shape, which the census above says is 84% of the bytes on the wire, is a near tie for the three serious tools, because at that point everybody is just moving bytes.

One choice worth declaring. The archives are packed with the compressor pinned to four threads. RAR's block split otherwise follows the core count of whatever machine packed the archive, so a 32-core box and a 20-core box produce different bytes from the same input and the machines stop being comparable. Pinning it makes the extraction corpus byte-identical everywhere, which is the point, but it also caps how much of the decode can run in parallel - so when the two closest shapes were losses we re-raced them against archives packed with all 32 threads, to check that the pinning was not what caused it. It was not: solid moved from 4.2% behind to 2.4% behind and the 128 MiB dictionary from 6.6% to 6.3%, the same ordering either way. Both are now wins on the pinned corpus by a wider margin than that check could account for.

Corpus: 1 GiB of random payload packed store-mode into 21 RAR volumes, then two PAR2 sets at 10% redundancy, one at 1 MiB blocks and one at 64 KiB, then fixed damage maps. Every run uses the same protocol: fresh copy, read the whole corpus once to warm the cache, then time. Best of three interleaved rounds; every repaired volume is compared against the pristine set on every round. Lower is better.

A correction about the corpus, because an earlier version of this page overstated it. We said every machine ran a byte-identical corpus, checked by hash. Hashing every volume of every set shows that is true of the 32-core desktop and the Windows laptop, which match exactly, and not of the 20-core desktop, which holds a different random draw of the same shape: the same 21 volumes at the same sizes, the same two block sizes, and damage verified at the same 3, 101 and 1,500 blocks spread across the same number of files. Every number within a row is still measured on bytes that every tool in that row shares, which is what each comparison rests on. But the rows are not four views of one input, and since payload character is worth about 7% to one competitor's scanning, that is worth stating rather than glossing.

Which par2 is which. The original par2cmdline is the reference implementation everyone forked from. par2cmdline-turbo is the fork that vendors ParPar's hand-written SIMD Galois-field kernels: that is precisely what the "turbo" means, and it is why turbo, rather than the original, is the tool worth measuring against. Both turbo columns below run those same ParPar kernels. What separates them is not the arithmetic but the build and the flags.

So the competition appears twice, and one of those columns is its best case rather than its default. The release binary you would download is compiled for a generic baseline CPU and hashes only a couple of files at a time; building the same source for the actual host CPU and passing -T16 lets it use the instructions that machine really has and hash sixteen files at once. On the laptop that is worth up to 2.6x, entirely from build and flags. Judge us on the tuned column, which is the harder comparison; the as-shipped column is what someone who downloads it actually experiences. par2cmdline is the original, version 1.2.0, built from source on each machine. rarpar is Weaver's own PAR2 implementation, built from source with its Metal GPU backend enabled. MultiPar's par2j is Windows-only, so it appears on the Windows laptop rows alone. The original is back in the table, and it is worth seeing why the fork exists. An earlier version of this page dropped the par2cmdline column on the grounds that it was slower than everything else in the round, which is true and is not a good enough reason: it is the implementation almost every other tool is descended from, and readers deserve the baseline rather than our assertion about it. On the heaviest damage level it takes about 69 s where the SIMD fork takes 3.2 s and we take 3.1 s. That factor of twenty is the whole argument for the hand-written Galois-field kernels, and it is the same argument we make for ours.

Light damage is the case that matters. A handful of failed articles is far more typical than 101 dead blocks, and nothing like 1,500. Most of a light repair is not the Reed-Solomon maths at all, it is reading and MD5-ing a gigabyte, which is why the 3-block row tracks the clean-verify row rather than the repair ones.

The heaviest row used to be the one we lose, and the previous cut of this page said the fix would be "a different algorithm rather than a tuning pass". This is that algorithm. That last level damages 1,500 blocks across all 21 volumes and consumes about 91% of the recovery data, which is where the Reed-Solomon arithmetic, rather than hashing or disk, becomes nearly all of the work. The shipping build now computes the heaviest repairs with a number-theoretic transform instead of the classic Galois-field fold - the same maths, evaluated in a form that scales far better at high block counts - and every heavy row flipped: 2.7× ahead of the tuned build on the 20-core desktop that was our closest loss, and on the Windows laptop that was our worst one, 2.7× ahead of the tuned build and 2.2× ahead of MultiPar. Light damage still runs the classic path, which is why the other rows moved barely at all: the transform only pays above about 512 damaged blocks, so below that the dispatcher does not use it.

A faster path is only worth having if it cannot be wrong. Both paths compute the same quantity and are bit-identical by construction, and every repair on this page was gated on the rebuilt files matching the pristine set: 228 timed repairs across the machines in this round, zero mismatches. The shipping build does not rely on that record. Every repair verifies its own output against the file hashes, and one that failed would be redone with the classic path automatically, log the divergence, and keep the classic path for the rest of that run. The setting is in the dashboard as Fast PAR mode if you would rather not have it at all, and machines with too little memory for it decline it on their own rather than trying and failing. Re-measured on 2 August on the current build: the two desktops land within a few percent of this table, and with Fast PAR mode switched off the 20-core desktop returns to exactly the loss it used to publish, which is what says the win is the method and not the conditions.

The laptop row needed a correction, and it goes against us. Windows demotes sustained background work onto its efficiency cores a few seconds in. Our daemon opts out of that at startup and none of the other tools can, so an earlier version of this page published their throttled times as though they were the tools' own. Rerunning that machine with every tool lifted to high priority moves the whole field: on the heaviest damage level par2-turbo goes from 22.4 s to 6.41 and rarpar from 59.2 s to 14.4, and for one cut of this page that turned the row from ours into one we lost. The whole laptop row is measured that way now - the correction stays even though the row has since been won back by the algorithm change above, because the field's times on that machine are only honest with the throttle lifted.

What moved this cut, and the control that says so. Finding which blocks are damaged had become the largest phase of this repair - larger than the repair arithmetic itself - and it ran on a single thread, reading 64 KB out of every group across the file, once per group. It now makes one sequential pass in file order with the per-shard checksums computed in parallel, and the repaired volume is cloned rather than copied where the filesystem can do that. Detection alone fell from about 300 ms to 18 ms on the 512 MB archive, which is most of what moved above. The file matching was never the cost, which is worth recording because it was the intuitive suspect: recovery volumes carry no filenames, so we identify which slots survived by checksumming every volume on disk rather than trusting what they are called, and against an undamaged set, where matching is all that happens, the whole pass takes 0.18 s.

What else moved, and where it does not show. Two more engine changes landed that these corpora cannot see, listed here so the numbers above are not read as the whole story: RAR5 archives with tens of thousands of members resolve each member once rather than walking the list per worker, which is 3× less processor time at 40,000 members; and the RAR1.3 bit reader works a word at a time, which is 2×. Neither appears above, because the shapes here have 400 members and no RAR1.3.

What we deliberately do not do: we never create PAR2. A downloader has no reason to, and ParPar owns that leg. We also buy speed with memory on both engines: extraction peaks around 240 MB against unrar's 41 MB, and verify around 126 MB against turbo's 7 MB, because these are the inline engines that ride a live download rather than standalone one-shots. The 128 MiB-dictionary shape is the worst of it, at about 423 MB against unrar's 139 MB. The heaviest repair now costs memory too: the faster method for 512-plus missing blocks works from the recovery data held resident, so it is allowed up to a quarter of the machine's RAM, capped at 4 GB, and a machine that cannot spare that quietly takes the low-memory method instead - the same arithmetic and the same times as the middle rows of the PAR2 table, just not the 3× on the last one. If you want the smallest possible resident set for a standalone job, the dedicated tools still win that column.

Methodology · in full, because we were asked

How we tested Weaver

Weaver's developer has said publicly that we avoided benchmarking it. The opposite is true: it appears in every comparison on this page that it can run, including the recovery-volume repair leg, which it wins and which we publish for exactly that reason. Here is the complete method, every issue we hit, and every workaround we used, so the runs can be reproduced and challenged.

  • What we ran: the official v0.7.5 arm64 release binary. Where a source build was needed (its own rarpar component for the PAR2 and extraction tables), we pin the exact commit next to the table.
  • Same conditions as everyone: identical machine, the same six commercial servers, TLS on every connection, jobs submitted through its NZBGet-compatible API, and the same pass rule as every client on this page: a leg counts only when the extracted payload is byte-verified on disk. Its own completion log is cross-checked, never trusted alone.
  • Measured at its best, not its worst: download legs marked warm run a persistent daemon, feed it a real warmup job first, and wait for its connection auto-ramp to reach its 120-connection ceiling before the timed job starts. Cold-start numbers, where shown, are labelled cold. We built and published the warm-start harness after observing that cold starts understate it.
  • Issues we hit, and what we did about them: one-shot download mode completes a job but never exits (we timed the daemon mode instead); headless Macs stall on a Keychain prompt (worked around with its encryption-key environment variable); a persistent duplicate-blocker database refuses resubmission of the same NZB even after a data wipe (worked around with its force flag); building from source fails without the separately built web frontend (stubbed; the official binary is unaffected); it refused our queue rig's self-signed certificate, so the queue leg above uses the clients that would connect; and one cold 190 GB run in July ended in a failed state after 33 minutes, so we re-ran it and published the completed rerun. Every workaround favours Weaver's result, not ours.
  • What the numbers say: it holds the same 2.0× peak disk as the other write-out-and-unpack clients, and its disk traffic grows faster than anyone's with job size (2.5× ours at 35 GB, 5.1× at 190 GB) because its encrypted-at-rest store re-reads and re-writes nearly everything it downloads. That design has real upsides that a speed page does not measure; the cost of it is what you see in the I/O column.
  • Reproduce it: the exact drivers, including the warm-start harness and its token handling, ship in our public repository's bench directory. Point them at your own servers and post what you get.

Transport proof

The engine saturates real lines

  • 8.31 Gbps aggregate on a measured-8.2 Gbps 10 GbE line (Europe M1 Ultra, 4 providers, 14,000 articles, 0 errors, 0 reconnects).
  • 9.16-9.27 Gbps steady transport ticks on the same line, ≈98% of practical TCP goodput.
  • 5.44 Gbps sustained through a full 20.4 GB job including in-stream verification.
  • Pipelining replicated at every RTT: +64% at 63 ms Starlink, +170-177% at 14 ms datacenter, +270% at 31 ms East Coast. It defeats TCP congestion-window decay, not just latency.
  • Backpressure proof: cap the write stage 4× below the network and input locks to the cap ±1% within 2 s, RAM falls, zero swaps. A slow disk can never balloon memory.
  • Compute headroom: measured decode+verify ceilings of 86-165 Gbps on Apple silicon. The network is the bottleneck by 4-50× on every machine tested.

rustnzb moves fastest of all - five releases between our pinned 1.3.4 (18 July) and 1.3.9 (28 July) - so we built 1.3.9 from source and re-raced it the same day. Its changelog promises fixes for exactly what our tables report ("make direct unpack reliable", "reject completed jobs without usable output"): measured, the obfuscated 35 GB release still arrives as raw volumes marked done, on both the single job and the queue. What did change is cost and pace: walls ~10% faster, and the processor-saturating churn we measured on 1.3.4 was absent from every 1.3.9 leg - though a same-day 1.3.4 re-run also measured low, so that cost is condition-dependent rather than version-fixed, and we quote it nowhere as a single number.
